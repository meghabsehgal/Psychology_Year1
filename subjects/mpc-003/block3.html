Gordon Allport — Dispositional Theory

Traits are real neuropsychic structures that initiate and guide consistent behaviour. Click any row to expand it.
▸ The Trait Hierarchy
🔴 Cardinal Trait
Dominates almost ALL behaviour — very rare
▶
A cardinal trait is so dominant that virtually everything a person does can be traced to its single influence. Very few people have a true cardinal trait — but when present, it colours their entire existence.
Example: Someone described as "Gandhian" — non-violence permeates every action. Historical names have become adjectives: Machiavellian (cunning), Don Quixote (idealism).
🟡 Central Traits
5–10 building blocks — easily recognised by others
▶
Central traits are the core descriptors of a person's personality — less all-consuming than cardinal traits but still quite generalised. When you write a reference letter, you list central traits.
Example: "She is outgoing, optimistic, kind, and hard-working." These 4–6 key descriptors are central traits — consistent across many situations but not every single one.
🟢 Secondary Traits
Specific, situational — only close friends notice them
▶
Secondary traits are less consistent, less generalised, and only visible in specific situations. You need to know someone very well to observe them.
Example: Gets nervous only during formal public presentations, or prefers a specific type of food — narrow, situational preferences that don't define the whole person.
📊 Common Traits vs Individual Traits
Cultural vs. uniquely personal traits
▶
Common (Nomothetic) Traits: Shared across a culture — allow comparison between people. E.g., anxiety, social attitude.

Individual (Idiographic) Traits: Unique to one person — cannot be used to compare. Found only through case histories, diaries, letters.
Allport argued the TRUE personality only emerges from individual study — group averages obscure what makes each person unique. He was critical of purely nomothetic approaches.
🔄 Functional Autonomy of Motives
Why adult motives don't need childhood roots
▶
Adult motives are INDEPENDENT of their original origins. You don't need to dig into someone's childhood to understand why they do something today. What was a means to an end becomes an end in itself.

Two types:
• Preservative: Repetitive neurological patterns (eating at same time daily)
• Propriate: Acquired interests, values, goals — the master motivation system
A student takes a field study because it is required → becomes absorbed in the topic for life. The original motive (grades) vanished; love of the subject is now self-sustaining.
🪞 The Proprium (Self) — 7 Stages
Allport's name for the self — develops from birth to adulthood
▶
The Proprium is Allport's term for the self — all aspects making a person unique. It has 7 sequential functions:

1. Bodily self — body sensations (infancy)
2. Self-identity — continuity through name and language (yr 1–2)
3. Self-esteem — pride in accomplishments (yr 3)
4. Self-extension — "mine" — body + environment + people (yr 4–6)
5. Self-image — "good me" vs "bad me" (yr 5–6)
6. Rational coping — solving problems with logic (yr 6–12)
7. Propriate striving — long-range goals, sense of purpose (adolescence → adulthood)
🌟 The Mature Personality — 6 Criteria
Allport's portrait of psychological health
▶
A mature person shows all six of these characteristics:

1. Extended sense of self — genuinely participates in work, relationships, society
2. Warm social interactions — intimacy with family + compassion for all humans
3. Emotional security and self-acceptance — handles frustration without hostility
4. Realistic perception — sees the world as it is, not as wished
5. Self-insight and humour — knows own strengths and weaknesses, can laugh at oneself
6. Unifying philosophy of life — a dominant goal or value gives life meaning and direction

Raymond Cattell — 16 Factor Theory

Factor analysis reveals 16 hidden source traits beneath observable behaviour.

📐 The Specification Equation

R = f ( S , P )

R = Response (behaviour)
S = Stimulus situation
P = Personality (all source traits weighted for that situation)

Behaviour is mathematically predictable if you know the situation AND all 16 source trait scores.

🧫 Surface vs Source Traits

Surface traits — observable clusters of co-occurring behaviours (e.g., restlessness + indecision → "neuroticism"). Not stable — no single underlying cause.

Source traits — deep causal building blocks. Stable across time and situations. Discovered ONLY via factor analysis. There are 16 of them.

📐 The 4-Level Hierarchy
From single acts to superfactors
▶
Eysenck arranged personality in 4 levels (bottom to top):

Level 1 — Specific responses: Talking to a specific friend on a single occasion
Level 2 — Habitual responses: Regularly joining clubs, talking to friends
Level 3 — Traits: Sociability, assertiveness, liveliness (components of Extraversion)
Level 4 — Superfactors/Types: Extraversion, Neuroticism, Psychoticism

Superfactors are stable and cross-situational. Specific behaviours vary day to day. Higher levels are more reliable for predicting behaviour.
🧠 Cortical Arousal & Extraversion (ARAS)
Why introverts avoid parties and extraverts seek them
▶
Extraversion is explained by the Ascending Reticular Activating System (ARAS) in the brain stem.

• Introverts: Chronically HIGHER baseline cortical arousal → already over-stimulated → avoid excitement
• Extraverts: Chronically LOWER baseline arousal → under-stimulated → constantly seek exciting situations

Based on the Yerkes-Dodson Law: performance is best at intermediate arousal. Both extremes cause performance to drop.
Lemon Drop Test (Corcoran, 1964): 4 drops of lemon juice on the tongue — introverts secrete nearly TWICE as much saliva as extraverts, showing their higher sensitivity to stimulation.
💛 Neuroticism & the Visceral Brain (Limbic System)
Emotional reactivity and the autonomic nervous system
▶
Neuroticism is linked to activation thresholds in the visceral brain (limbic system) — hippocampus, amygdala, septum, hypothalamus.

• High Neuroticism: Lower thresholds → reacts quickly and persistently to even minor stressors
• Emotionally Stable: Higher thresholds → calm under stress, quick recovery

Measured via: heart rate, blood pressure, skin conductance, breathing rate, muscular tension.
High Introversion + High Neuroticism → prone to anxiety disorders (phobias, OCD). High Extraversion + High Neuroticism → increased risk for antisocial/psychopathic behaviour.
⚗️ Psychoticism & Gonadal Hormones
Testosterone, MAO enzyme, and the P dimension
▶
Psychoticism is biologically linked to testosterone and low levels of the enzyme monoamine oxidase (MAO).

Low platelet MAO found in psychotic patients AND their relatives — suggesting it's a biological vulnerability marker.

High P scorers: troublesome, uncooperative, egocentric, hostile, impulsive, non-conformist. More common in men than women.
Eysenck added Psychoticism as a 3rd dimension in 1976 — seen as a genetic predisposition toward psychosis OR psychopathy placed on a continuum, not an all-or-nothing category.

📜 The Lexical Hypothesis

The most important personality differences get encoded as words in natural language over generations. Starting from Allport & Odbert's ~18,000 English trait words → Cattell's 4,500 → 35 clusters → 5 factors. The Big Five has been replicated in German, Chinese, Dutch, and many other languages.

✅ 6 Key Features of the Big Five

1. Dimensional — continuous, not categories
2. Stable — consistent across 45 years
3. Heritable — partly genetic
4. Adaptive — evolutionary value
5. Universal — found across cultures
6. Clinically useful — aids therapeutic insight
